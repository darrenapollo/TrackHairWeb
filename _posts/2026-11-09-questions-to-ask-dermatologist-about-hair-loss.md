A hair loss appointment can be short, and it's easy to come out having forgotten the question you most wanted to ask. A little preparation helps. If you arrive with a rough timeline, a few comparable photos and your questions written down, the clinician spends less time reconstructing what happened and more time on what to do next.

This is general information, not medical advice.

## Who to see first

In the UK, the NHS suggests seeing a GP if you're worried about your hair loss, and seeing a GP to get an idea of the cause before you go to a commercial hair clinic. A GP may be able to tell what's causing it by looking at your hair. In other countries the first stop may be a family doctor or a dermatologist directly. The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) notes that seeing a board-certified dermatologist helps when you need an accurate diagnosis.

Either way, the preparation below is the same.

## What you'll probably be asked

The AAD says it's important to know how long you've had hair loss and whether it came on quickly. Expect versions of these questions:

- When did you first notice it, and has it changed since?
- Did it start gradually, or suddenly?
- Where is it happening: the hairline, the crown, the part, all over, or in patches?
- Are you shedding more hairs than usual, or is the hair getting thinner and shorter?
- Has anything changed in the months before it started, such as an illness, a big change in weight, or a stressful period?
- What do you currently take or apply, including supplements and anything bought without a prescription?
- Does anyone in your family have similar hair loss?

The NHS lists illness, stress, cancer treatment, weight loss and iron deficiency among the causes of temporary hair loss, and notes that pattern baldness usually runs in the family. That's why questions about your recent history and your family come up so often.

The AAD also describes what the exam usually involves. A dermatologist looks closely at your scalp, your nails and any other area with hair loss, and may gently pull on your hair to see how it's growing and whether it breaks easily. If they suspect a disease, a vitamin deficiency, a hormone imbalance or an infection, they may suggest a blood test or a scalp biopsy.

## What to bring

### A short timeline

Write down when you first noticed a change, how quickly it happened, and any dates that might matter: an illness, a new medication, childbirth, a big change in diet or weight. Keep it to a few lines. Rough dates are fine. "Around March" is better than nothing.

### Comparable photos

The mirror is a poor guide to slow change. Photos help, but only if they're taken the same way each time: the same light, distance and angles, with your hair in the same condition. Three or four sets spaced over a few months are more useful than a camera roll of random selfies. If you haven't started yet, take a baseline set now using the [progress photo guide](/hair-loss-progress-photos/). Even one set gives you something to compare with at a follow-up.

### A list of what you use

Include everything: prescriptions, products from a pharmacy, supplements, shampoos and anything you've tried and stopped. Note roughly when you started and stopped each one, and how consistently you used it. If you've been following a routine for a while, the [treatment progress guide](/blog/how-to-track-hair-loss-treatment-progress/) shows how to keep that record in order.

### Notes on shedding and side effects

If you've noticed heavier shedding, an itchy or sore scalp, or anything else that worries you, write down when it happened. A dated note is easier to discuss than a memory of "a few weeks ago".

## Questions to ask

Choose the ones that matter to you and write them down before you go:

1. What do you think is causing my hair loss, and how sure can you be from today's exam?
2. Do I need any tests, and what would they look for?
3. Is this likely to be temporary or long term?
4. What are my options, including doing nothing for now?
5. For any option you suggest, how long before we can judge whether it's helping?
6. What side effects should I watch for, and when should I get in touch?
7. Should I change anything I'm currently taking or applying?
8. How should I track changes between now and the next appointment?
9. When should I come back, and what would make you want to see me sooner?

Question 5 is easy to forget and saves a lot of worry later. The right review window depends on the diagnosis and the treatment, and asking up front gives you a date to work toward instead of checking the mirror every morning.

The NHS also suggests telling your GP if your hair loss is affecting your wellbeing. If it is, say so. They may be able to help you get some support.

## After the appointment

Write down what was said while you still remember it: the likely cause, any tests, the plan, and the date of your next review. Then start the record that will make the next appointment easier. Take a photo set on the same day, note the plan you were given, and put the review date in your calendar.

At the follow-up, you can then compare like with like: photos from the same angles, a log of what you actually did, and notes on anything that happened in between.
