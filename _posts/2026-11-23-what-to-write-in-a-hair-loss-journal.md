Hair photos taken months apart are hard to read on their own. One looks thinner, and you can't remember whether you'd just washed it, whether it was taken under the bathroom light or by the window, or whether that was the week you were ill. A short journal next to the photos answers those questions. It doesn't need to be long, and it works best when it isn't.

This guide covers what to note when you take your check-in photos, what to log in between, and a template you can copy. If you're tracking a treatment, [how to track hair loss treatment progress](/blog/how-to-track-hair-loss-treatment-progress/) goes further into doses, review dates and clinician notes. This page is about the journal itself.

## Why the notes matter more than you'd expect

Most of the difference between two hair photos taken a few weeks apart comes from the conditions, not the hair. Overhead light shows more scalp than daylight from a window. Wet hair clumps together and shows more scalp than dry hair. A fresh haircut makes the hair shorter and the scalp more visible. A camera held closer makes everything look thinner.

If you write those conditions down, you can tell later whether two photos are fair to compare. If you don't, you end up comparing a good photo with a bad one and reading meaning into it. The [progress photos guide](/hair-loss-progress-photos/) covers how to keep the conditions the same. The journal records when they weren't.

## What to note at each check-in

Write these next to every set of photos. It takes a minute.

The room and the light. "Bathroom, overhead light" or "bedroom, window, morning" is enough. If you can, use the same one every time and note when you couldn't.

Your hair that day. Washed or not, wet or dry, any product, and how it was styled. Wash day alone can change how much scalp shows.

Anything that changed since the last check-in. A haircut (and roughly how short), a new shampoo, a change to a routine you follow, a new medication from your doctor. Date it.

How the photos were taken. Same distance and height as before, or different. If someone else took the top photo this time, say so.

## What to log between check-ins

Between check-ins, the journal is mostly short lines on the days something happened.

The days you followed your routine, and the days you missed. Gaps belong in the record. A routine followed for three months and one followed for three weeks out of twelve are different situations, and the photos alone won't tell you which you had.

Illness, a high fever, surgery, a very hard few weeks, or a big change in diet or weight. The American Academy of Dermatology notes that shedding after a stressful event such as an illness or giving birth can start a few months later, so the date of the event is worth having when you look back. It's also useful to bring to a clinician.

Shedding you noticed, described plainly. "More in the shower this week" is more useful than a guess at a number. The AAD puts normal shedding at 50 to 100 hairs a day, so a few hairs on the pillow is expected.

Sleep, if it's been unusually bad for a stretch. One line is plenty.

## What to leave out

How your hair looked in the lift mirror. Whether today felt like a bad hair day. Daily verdicts mostly record the light and your mood, and reading them back later is discouraging without being informative. Save the looking for the check-in photos, on the date you chose.

## A template to copy

At each check-in:

- Date:
- Room and light:
- Hair: washed / not, wet / dry, styled how:
- Changes since last check-in:
- Photos taken the same way as last time? Yes / no, and what was different:

Between check-ins, one line when something happens:

- Date, what happened (missed routine, ill, haircut, more shedding, bad sleep week)

That's the whole journal. It should take less time than the photos.

## How often to look back

Hair changes slowly, so compare check-ins a few months apart rather than week to week. When you do look back, read the notes first. If two check-ins were taken in different light or one was just after a haircut, take that into account before you decide anything changed. For the crown in particular, [how to photograph and track crown thinning](/blog/how-to-photograph-crown-thinning/) has the setup and the comparison steps.

A journal can't tell you why your hair is changing. It keeps the timing so you and a clinician can look at it together. The NHS advises seeing a GP if you have sudden hair loss, bald patches, or hair loss that worries you.
