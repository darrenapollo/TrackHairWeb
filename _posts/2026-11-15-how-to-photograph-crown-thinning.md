Most people check their hair in a bathroom mirror, which shows the hairline and the temples well and the top of the head not at all. The crown, the area at the back of the top of your head where the hair grows in a swirl, is the part you only see in a lift mirror, a holiday photo or a friend's phone. That's why crown changes so often come as a surprise.

Photographing it on purpose, the same way each time, takes the surprise out of it. This guide covers how to take a crown photo you can compare months later, the conditions that make it look worse than it is, and how often to look.

This is general information, not medical advice.

## Why the crown matters

The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) describes hereditary hair loss in men as often showing up as a receding hairline or a bald spot at the top of the head, and in women as thinning over the top of the scalp. The NHS describes male-pattern baldness in a similar way. So the crown is one of the first places pattern hair loss can appear, and the one you're least likely to notice early.

Seeing a change in a photo doesn't tell you the cause. If you're worried, the NHS suggests seeing a GP, and a dermatologist can examine the scalp properly. Good photos make that conversation easier. The [questions to ask a dermatologist](/blog/questions-to-ask-dermatologist-about-hair-loss/) post covers what to bring.

## How to take a crown photo you can compare

### Camera position

Hold the phone above and slightly behind your head with the screen facing up and the camera facing down. Arm straight, phone flat. If you can, ask someone else to take it from the same height each time; it's much easier to keep consistent.

If you're on your own, use the self-timer or burst mode and take several. Pick the one where the swirl is in the middle of the frame. Some people use a second mirror behind them and photograph the reflection, but the angle changes more from one session to the next, so a direct photo is better if you can manage it.

### Hair

Dry, unstyled and combed the way you normally wear it. Wet hair clumps together and shows a lot more scalp, so a crown photo straight out of the shower will look thinner than the same crown an hour later. Product, a fresh cut and a different parting all change the picture too. Pick one state and keep it: for most people that's dry hair on the same day after washing.

### Light

Light is the biggest source of false alarms. A strong light directly overhead lights up the scalp through the hair and makes thin areas look much thinner. Soft, even light from a window is kinder and, more usefully, easier to repeat. Choose one room and one time of day, and don't use the flash.

### Distance and framing

Keep the phone at about the same distance each time, so the crown fills roughly the same part of the frame. Close-ups exaggerate gaps between hairs. A frame that shows the crown and a bit of the surrounding hair gives you something to compare against.

## What changes the picture

When two crown photos look different, check these before you conclude anything about your hair:

| What changed | What it does |
|---|---|
| Wet or dry hair | Wet hair shows far more scalp |
| Overhead or window light | Overhead light makes thinning look worse |
| Camera height | Closer looks sparser and bigger |
| Haircut | Shorter hair covers less scalp |
| Parting or styling | Moves where the scalp shows |
| Wash day | Day-two hair sits differently from fresh hair |

Shedding is another thing that worries people. The AAD says it's normal to shed between 50 and 100 hairs a day, so hairs in the drain or on the pillow are not, on their own, a sign of thinning.

## How often to compare

Hair grows slowly, so comparing today's crown photo with yesterday's mostly shows you the light. A more useful rhythm:

1. Take a baseline set: front hairline, top of the scalp and the crown, plus a side profile.
2. Write down how you took them: the room, the light, wet or dry, the distance.
3. Set a check-in date a few months away and repeat the same set the same way.
4. Compare the crown with the crown, at the same angle, side by side.

If you're tracking a routine your clinician suggested, note the date you started and any changes. Photos are much easier to read when you know what you were doing during the months between them. The [how to track hair loss treatment progress](/blog/how-to-track-hair-loss-treatment-progress/) post goes through that in more detail, and the [progress photos guide](/hair-loss-progress-photos/) covers the other angles.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing a holiday photo with a bathroom photo. Different light, different everything.
- Taking the crown photo with wet hair one month and dry the next.
- Zooming in to count hairs. Gaps look bigger the closer you get.
- Checking every morning. The day-to-day differences are almost all conditions, and they're unsettling for no reason.
- Only photographing the front. The crown is where a lot of people notice change last.
